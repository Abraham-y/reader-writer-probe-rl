# A Probe's AUROC Belongs to a Population, Not to the Probe: 
Measurement Validity for Interpretability Artifacts Used as Control Signals

## Abstract

A linear probe on a $0.5$B policy's hidden states predicts whether the answer it is about to give will be correct, reaching held-out AUROC $0.982$. Promote a probe of this kind to the RL reward and true accuracy falls $31$ percentage points. Our question is whether the monitor's own accuracy shows it. It does not. Scored at every checkpoint against fresh answers from that same checkpoint, the probe's AUROC is unchanged for about **40 RLOO steps** after the policy has begun gaming it, and breaks only at step 50. An LLM judge watching the same collapse ends where it began ($0.785 \to 0.769$), with its prevalence-invariant likelihood ratio flat ($2.46 \to 2.57$) across a $31.7$\,pp drop in accuracy. There the reason is visible, because we can verify the judge is a fixed function: of the 965 cases it scored more than once, exactly one changes verdict. Its health was never the variable. What moved was the population it was handed. This is *spectrum bias*, familiar from diagnostic testing, with the causation reversed: deploying an interpretability artifact as a control signal is what destroys the distribution its read-only number was measured on. The one statistic that departs early, $\sim$40 steps before AUROC, is also the only one needing no labels: the share of answers passing a frozen threshold.

Read-only quality does not merely fail to transfer. It orders the outcomes **backwards**. A monitor reading no activations at all, only 39 cheap features of the answer text, scores $0.925$ as a reader and drives accuracy to exactly $\mathbf{0.0000}$ as a reward, with $99.9\%$ of answers still well-formed. Scored on one population the three rewards rank inversely to how well they read: $0.834 \to 0.168$, $0.861 \to 0.073$, $0.925 \to 0.000$. Nothing in the objective rewards hiding from the monitor, so this is not evasion; the policy is doing what the monitor asks. A better reader is not a safer writer.

## Introduction

A probe is a small classifier trained on a model's internal activations to predict something about what the model is doing. Probes are increasingly proposed not as analysis tools but as control signals: refusal directions \citep{arditi2024refusal}, correctness probes \citep{burns2022discovering}, deception probes \citep{pacchiardi2023lie}. The obvious next step is to put one inside the training loop and optimize against it. That step is much less well understood than reading a probe from the outside, and it is the subject of this paper.

Two literatures bear on it. Reward overoptimization \citep{gao2023scaling, coste2023reward, skalse2022defining, denison2024sycophancy} shows that a proxy reward gets gamed once a policy can push on it, with no adversarial intent anywhere. Monitor evasion \citep{baker2025monitoring, gupta2025rlobfuscation, taufeeque2026atlas, bailey2024obfuscated} shows that monitors reading internal states can be evaded when the monitor enters as a penalty. A third, on LLM evaluators, reaches our conclusion from the measurement side: agreement statistics overstate judge reliability \citep{zheng2023judging, norman2026reliability, weng2026invariance}, and reward-model benchmark scores predict downstream outcomes weakly \citep{yang2026auditing, frick2024ppe, malik2025rewardbench2}.

We claim neither mechanism as novel. What we add is the dynamic case: what a monitor's own quality statistics do *while* the policy it watches degrades. \citet{baker2025monitoring} report monitor recall collapsing under optimization pressure but cannot separate two explanations, because the monitor is not scored on held-apart data: either the agent learned to evade it, or it stopped discriminating. A task with an exact verifier separates them. At every checkpoint we score the monitor against ground truth on answers drawn from that checkpoint, and the answer is that a monitor's health statistics are the last thing to move. This mirrors \citet{beigi2026prime}, who show that probing a policy internally forecasts reward hacking before the visible hack rate rises: the information is there to be read, just not in the monitor's own accuracy against its target.

This matters for interpretability specifically. AUROC, precision and recall are not properties of a classifier; they are properties of a classifier together with a population, and the field reports probe AUROC as though it belonged to the probe. \citet{belinkov2022probing} argues that probe accuracy does not license a claim about whether the model *uses* the direction. We show something adjacent: it does not license a claim about the probe either, because deploying the artifact changes the population the number was measured on. Our judge makes this concrete: a deterministic function that never changes, whose AUROC barely moves, while the policy underneath it collapses.

We work on Qwen2.5-0.5B trained on Countdown arithmetic \citep{gandhi2024stream} with RLOO \citep{ahmadian2024back}. The task has an exact verifier, so a learned scorer's divergence from ground truth is measurable directly, without preference-model confounds.

\paragraph{Contributions.} One claim, in two halves.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item **A monitor's read-only quality does not survive its promotion to a target, and gets the order backwards.** Our reader probe reaches AUROC $0.982$; a *different* probe, sharing a direction with it at cosine only $0.163$, costs $31$\,pp when it becomes the reward. A monitor reading only cheap text features reads at $0.925$ and drives accuracy to exactly $\mathbf{0.0000}$, with $99.9\%$ of answers still well-formed. Scored on one population the three rewards rank inversely to how well they read: $0.834 \to 0.168$, $0.861 \to 0.073$, $0.925 \to 0.000$. (\S\ref{sec:probe-rl}, \S\ref{sec:surface})
  \item **The monitor's own statistics will not tell you, because they were never about the monitor alone.** Scored at every checkpoint on that checkpoint's own answers, the probe's AUROC is unchanged through step 40 while the policy has been gaming it since step 10. An LLM judge watching the same collapse ends where it began, and there the reason is visible: the judge is a fixed function, so its flat AUROC was never a fact about the judge. Discrimination belongs to a classifier and a population jointly, and deploying the classifier is what moves the population. The one statistic that departs early, $\sim$40 steps before AUROC, is also the only one needing no labels: the share of answers passing a frozen threshold. (\S\ref{sec:lag}, \S\ref{sec:judge})
\end{enumerate}

## Setup {#sec:setup}

Qwen2.5-0.5B on Countdown: given 3--4 integers and a target, produce an equation using each exactly once, with an exact rule-based verifier. $C_{\text{SFT}}$ is a supervised checkpoint; $C_{\text{outcome}}$ is 100 RLOO steps against the verifier, taking pass@1 from $28.6\%$ to $53.5\%$. Eval is 406 procedurally generated problems with no overlap against the RLOO training pool. AUROC here is the probability that a randomly chosen correct answer scores above a randomly chosen incorrect one, so $0.5$ is chance; it is seed-dependent at the $\sim$0.015 level and we do not lean on a third decimal. Appendix~\ref{app:setup} gives probes, hyperparameters and the estimator.

**Who is who.** Four objects in this paper could reasonably be called ``the monitor'', and two of them share a name in the literature. Table~\ref{tab:actors} fixes the names.

## The probe is a good reader {#sec:probe-reader}

A premise, not a contribution: the monitor we go on to misuse is one worth deploying. A trace-final probe on layer-16 \texttt{</think>} states reaches held-out AUROC $\mathbf{0.982}$ on $C_{\text{outcome}}$, and as a best-of-16 selector captures $88.8\%$ of the accuracy gain available to any selector (CI $[78.9\%, 96.5\%]$). Free baselines come close, which matters in \S\ref{sec:surface}: \texttt{</think>} position alone scores $0.915$ and a 39-feature surface model $0.926$, and the probe beats each by $+3.20$\,pp of selection accuracy. It is a good detector that partly rides on a cheap surface correlate, and it is not merely a length detector. Appendix~\ref{app:reader} gives the baselines, the length controls, and what this does and does not license.

**There are two different probes in this paper.** The probe above is the *reader* and is never a reward. The probe used as the reward in \S\ref{sec:probe-rl}, and scored at every checkpoint in \S\ref{sec:lag}, is a different fit on temperature-1 samples, with its own held-out AUROC of $\mathbf{0.810}$; they share a direction at cosine only $0.163$. One disclosure: $248$ of its $300$ fitting prompts are among the $406$ evaluated in \S\ref{sec:lag}, so that evaluation is not prompt-disjoint from the fit. Answers are freshly sampled at every checkpoint and its step-0 reading of $0.783$ sits *below* its held-out $0.810$, which is not what leakage produces, but the populations do overlap and we would rather say so.

## Using the probe as the reward destroys the task {#sec:probe-rl}

So far the probe has only been read. Now we make it the thing the model is trained to maximize. The reward is the probe's $P(\text{correct})$, read at \texttt{</think>} from a frozen copy of the probe reloaded each round. The verifier still runs, but only so we can log true accuracy; it does not enter the gradient. Everything else matches the vanilla RL run: 100 RLOO steps, identical hyperparameters.

We ran this from two different starting points, because where you start turns out to matter more than anything else we varied:
\begin{description}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item[\texttt{runA}] Starts from $C_{\text{outcome}}$, so the probe is in-distribution at step 0.
  \item[\texttt{runB}] Starts from $C_{\text{SFT}}$, so the probe is out-of-distribution at step 0.
\end{description}

\paragraph{Downstream eval.} On the clean-406 set, first-block accuracy: \texttt{runA} falls to $\mathbf{0.236}$, which is $-31$\,pp from $C_{\text{outcome}}$'s $0.550$, and \texttt{runB} to $\mathbf{0.073}$, $-22$\,pp from $C_{\text{SFT}}$'s $0.290$. Both runs reached a probe score of about $0.99$ while doing it, and they got there by opposite routes: \texttt{runA} learned to ramble, \texttt{runB} to commit early.

\paragraph{What the trained policy writes.} The answers acquire the form of careful reasoning and lose its content. A transparent seven-marker template score rises $2.00 \to 4.32$ between the vanilla checkpoint and \texttt{runB}, while its correlation with actually being correct collapses from $r{=}{+}0.320$ to $+0.025$. The probe goes on scoring them as correct. \S\ref{sec:surface} shows what this looks like verbatim.

\subsection{The probe's own accuracy stays intact for 40 steps after the gaming starts}\label{sec:lag}

Here is the question this section answers. The policy is being trained against the probe and is getting worse at the actual task. If you were watching the probe's accuracy, when would you notice?

We can ask this precisely because the monitor is a correctness probe on a verifiable task, which the reward-model literature cannot do: a reward model would need fresh human preference labels at every checkpoint, whereas we already have the verifier. We take all eleven checkpoints of the probe-as-reward run. At each one we sample 8 fresh answers per prompt *from that checkpoint*, score them with the frozen reward probe, and compare against ground truth. The probe is the only thing held fixed across checkpoints; the answers it is scoring are different every time, because the policy writing them has changed. Figure~\ref{fig:lag} is the result, and Table~\ref{tab:lag} in Appendix~\ref{app:ladders} gives every checkpoint.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.82\linewidth]{figures/lag.pdf}
\caption{The warning you do not get. **Top:** true accuracy falls from step 10, while the fraction of answers clearing a threshold frozen at step 0 rises. The policy is gaming the monitor throughout the shaded span. **Bottom:** the monitor's own discrimination over the same span, scored at each checkpoint on that checkpoint's freshly sampled answers, is indistinguishable from where it started until step 50. Both panels and the interval are drawn from the same data as Table~\ref{tab:lag}.}
\label{fig:lag}
\end{figure}

**Part of that flatness is the metric, not the policy.** AUROC depends only on the order the probe puts answers in, and between steps 0 and 40 the mean score inflates $0.460 \to 0.677$ while leaving that order almost intact. The obvious replacements do not rescue you: precision at a frozen threshold is confounded with the falling share of correct answers, and the prevalence-invariant $\mathrm{LR}+$ falls $2.41 \to 1.65$, *more* than raw precision rather than less. Appendix~\ref{app:metrics} works this through.

**What warns you without labels.** AUROC, precision and $\mathrm{LR}+$ all need to know which answers were correct, and if you had that you could measure the accuracy drop directly. Score *saturation* does not work either: the attacked run has $36.7\%$ of answers above $0.95$ by step 40, but the verifier-RL control reaches $37.2\%$ by step 60 while its accuracy is rising. **Flag rate does work.** Freeze a threshold and watch what share of answers clear it. Under attack it rises monotonically ($+6\%$, $+12\%$, $+20\%$, $\mathbf{+38\%}$ at steps 10 to 40, eventually $+99\%$) and never returns. Every checkpoint is scored on the same 406 prompts, so the right test is paired, and it fires at the first checkpoint after training begins: $+0.030\,[+0.012,+0.048]$ at **step 10** ($p=0.001$; one bootstrap draw of 2{,}000 crossed zero), against an AUROC break at step 50. That is a label-free lead of about **40 steps**, growing to $+0.496$ by step 99.

**Part of this is definitional and we would rather say so.** The probe's flag rate is the threshold-crossing rate of the probe score, and the probe score *is* the training objective. Gradient ascent pushes it up by construction, so an early departure is partly guaranteed rather than discovered. What is not guaranteed is the *gap*: that the same score can inflate for 40 steps while the ranking it induces stays intact. The clean case is the judge in \S\ref{sec:judge}, which the policy was never trained against and whose flag rate moves anyway. That result is weaker (step 20, $p=0.013$) and it is the one to lean on.

**The control is the weakest part of this paper.** A paired test against step 0 shows the statistic *moved*, not that it moved more than benign training moves it, and our control ladder for that has four defects, set out in Appendix~\ref{app:metrics}. Two independent routes through it give step 10 and only the contaminated one gives step 30. Read the lead as evidence that the ordering exists, not as a calibrated false-alarm rate; a matched control ladder is the highest-value experiment left undone here.

We claim the RL-training characterisation, not the alarm itself. Watching a score distribution against a frozen reference is standard production monitoring, and \citet{li2026whodrifted} give a better instrument for it: anytime-valid e-processes with an anchor set, which additionally attribute drift to the system or to the judge. What we add is a price, in RLOO steps, for what that alarm buys against a monitor whose AUROC has not moved.

\subsection{Why: a monitor that provably did not change}\label{sec:judge}

\S\ref{sec:lag} leaves an ambiguity we cannot resolve with the probe alone. The probe's AUROC stayed flat while the policy collapsed, but the probe was the training target, so one can always argue the probe did change in some way relative to the answers it was scoring. To separate the monitor from the population we need a monitor that is *incapable* of changing, watching the same collapse. That is what this section is: not a second experiment, but the control that says which of the two moved.

We use an LLM judge, which is also what practitioners actually deploy. We re-sample all eleven checkpoints of the same run (406 prompts $\times$ 8 answers, 35{,}728 in total) and score every answer twice: with the exact verifier, and with \texttt{Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct} shown the problem and the proposed equation and asked whether it is correct. The judge's score is $P(\textsc{yes})$ renormalised over the two verdict tokens. It is genuinely imperfect: at step 0 it reaches precision $0.746$ at its own median threshold against a true accuracy of $0.544$. The policy was never optimised against it.

**The judge is a fixed function, and we checked.** Of the 965 (problem, equation) pairs it scored more than once, exactly one changes which side of the threshold it falls on, and of the 48 it scored at both step 0 and step 99, none do. Whatever its statistics do below, they are not the judge changing.

**And its statistics barely move** (Table~\ref{tab:judge}). Paired prompt-clustered bootstraps show a small dip at steps 20--40 ($-0.031$ at step 30, $p = 0.002$) and a recovery by step 80. Between the first and last checkpoints there is no difference: the paired change is $-0.017$, $95\%$ CI $[-0.048, +0.014]$, which bounds any degradation at about $0.05$ rather than merely failing to reject zero. The largest excursion anywhere is $0.031$ of AUROC, arriving while true accuracy falls $0.544 \to 0.457$, and it would not trip any plausible alert. Raw precision does fall, $0.746 \to 0.429$, but the share of answers that are correct falls $0.544 \to 0.226$ over the same span and precision at a fixed threshold moves with that share by construction. The prevalence-invariant statistic, $\mathrm{LR}+ = \mathrm{TPR}/\mathrm{FPR}$, is flat end to end at $\mathbf{2.46 \to 2.57}$. (For the same reason, do not use *lift*: it is not prevalence-invariant, and $\sim$91\% of its apparent improvement here is the confound it appears to remove, with roughly $70\%$ of the flag rate's own movement predicted the same way.) Across 41 paired contrasts, Bonferroni over the ten judge AUROC contrasts leaves only step 30, and the judge's flag-rate departure at step 20 ($p=0.013$) does not survive; both tables report nominal $p$.

**So the flat AUROC was never a fact about the judge.** Its health was not the variable. What moved was the population it was handed: correct answers fell from $54.4\%$ of the pool to $22.6\%$, illegal ones grew from $28\%$ of the wrong answers to $43\%$, and the surface form changed. The policy stopped using parentheses, the share of correct answers with none rising $0.017 \to 0.982$, and the judge is sensitive to exactly that: on the same expression differing only in bracketing it passes $0.746$ with parentheses and $0.548$ without ($+0.198$, $95\%$ CI $[+0.131, +0.265]$). The judge is riding a surface correlate too. Sensitivity and specificity belong to a classifier *and a population together*, so nothing in a monitor's own label-requiring statistics would have warned you the policy was failing. None of this is new as statistics: it is *spectrum bias*, described for diagnostic tests by \citet{ransohoff1978spectrum} and standard in clinical epidemiology since. What is new here is the setting and the direction of causation. In the clinical case the population varies for reasons outside the test; here the artifact's own deployment is what moves the population, so the number an interpretability paper reports read-only is measured on the one distribution that deploying it destroys.

**And the label-free statistic moves here too, in the opposite direction.** The flag rate falls $0.500 \to 0.317$, $-37\%$ against AUROC's $-2\%$, departing at step 20 ($-0.026\,[-0.047,-0.007]$, $p=0.013$) though not at step 10 ($p=0.069$), and it is not monotone, bottoming at $0.294$ by step 80 while accuracy still falls. This is the non-circular case: the probe's flag rate is the threshold-crossing rate of the RL objective itself, so its early departure is partly guaranteed, whereas the judge was never optimised against. What to watch is *departure* from the frozen operating point, not a direction. \citet{li2026whodrifted} give a better instrument for that than ours, anytime-valid e-processes with an anchor set; our contribution is the price in RLOO steps, not the alarm.

**One scope limit.** The judge is a bystander, so this is not a demonstration that a judge can be gamed; \citet{zhou2026convincing}, who optimise against judges directly, report discrimination collapsing (TPR$-$FPR $0.311 \to 0.086$). A bystander judge instead keeps its discrimination while the policy collapses underneath. Neither warns you, and the bystander case is the more common one.

\subsection{How much of this is the policy occupying cheap surface features?}\label{sec:surface}

The probe partly rides on surface properties of the text (\S\ref{sec:probe-reader}). A natural hypothesis is that the collapse happens because the policy learns to manufacture those surface properties. We test the hypothesis in two directions: first with detectors that read nothing but the text, then with reward arms that were pre-registered before we ran them.

**Then we closed the loop with two pre-registered RL arms**, predictions committed before launch. Arm A borrows the logic of amnesic probing \citep{elazar2021amnesic}, though not its method: we residualise the 39 surface features out of the activations by least squares, refit on what remains, and train against that, which asks whether the collapse needs the surface information the probe rode on. Elazar et al.\ remove a property by iterative nullspace projection and measure the behavioural counterfactual; we remove a hand-specified feature set and measure a downstream training outcome. Both arms use the same recipe as the published run, both initialised from $C_{\text{SFT}}$ and compared against the init-matched run \texttt{runB} at $0.0734$, which we list as arm C. These three are rankable against each other and against nothing else in this paper (Table~\ref{tab:arms}).

{{TABLE:tab:arms}}

**Arm B is the sharpest result in the paper.** A reward with no access to the model's internals drove accuracy to exactly zero, and the output is not degenerate: $100\%$ of answers still emit \texttt{</think>} and $99.9\%$ a well-formed \texttt{<answer>} block. Verbatim, for input $[29, 17, 63, 38]$ and target $68$:

\vspace{-0.35em}
\begin{quote}\ttfamily\small\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
<answer>\\
Let me verify:\\
1. 17 - 10 = 7\\
2. 63 - 7 = 56\\
3. 29 * 38 = 1102\\
4. 56 - (29 * 38 / 38) = 68\\
</answer>
\end{quote}
\vspace{-0.35em}

Step 1 uses $10$, which was not in the input. Step 3 is correct and irrelevant. Step 4 reuses two numbers and evaluates to 27, while asserting 68 in the answer position. The answer has the shape of careful verification and contains no valid arithmetic. Mean length went from $1{,}098$ to $2{,}390$ characters, because the reward's features \texttt{ans\_len}, \texttt{ans\_ops} and \texttt{n\_equals} pay for exactly that.

Arm A confirmed its pre-registered prediction against the init-matched control, collapsing $9.4$\,pp less than the raw-probe run. Comparing it instead against \texttt{runA}, which starts from $C_{\text{outcome}}$, reverses the sign: initialisation alone is worth about $16$\,pp here, twice the effect being measured, which is why the comparison must match on it.

\paragraph{What this licenses, and what it does not.} It licenses this: occupying cheap textual structure is *sufficient* to destroy both a monitor and the task, with no internal access needed. It does not license the claim that this is *the* mechanism behind the published probe's collapse, and arm A cuts against the simple version of that story.

The prescription is narrower. Before wiring any monitor into a gradient, measure how much of its AUROC survives conditioning on cheap surface features. Ours survives well, holding $0.982 \to 0.939$ under \texttt{</think>}-position stratification, and it collapsed anyway, because the axes the *policy* can reach are the surface ones. The question is not what the monitor can see. It is what the optimizer can reach.

## Limitations

**Every RL configuration is a single seed**, including the ladder the lag is measured on; our three analysis seeds bound measurement noise, not training variance. These are demonstrations of direction, not estimates of size. Scale runs the unhelpful way: 0.5B throughout, and the 1.5B replication holds as a reader (AUROC $0.974$) while the applied gain *shrinks*, $+8.6$ against $+11.7$\,pp. One task, one model family, one RL algorithm, one bystander judge.

**No causal experiment, and a withdrawn result.** An earlier version reported that RL renders the probe direction causally active under steering. We do not: the steered vector has cosine $0.163$ with the probe actually optimised rather than the $1.000$ that claim assumed, the intervention landed one transformer block and 2--3 tokens downstream of the read site, and the key contrasts reach only $p=0.063$. Those checkpoints are deleted. A defensible version needs interventions aligned to the read site, evaluated as a causal abstraction \citep{geiger2021abstractions}.

**Reproducibility.** Every number regenerates on CPU from cached artifacts. One script prints each published value beside its recomputation, covering this paper's prose, its tables and the three AUROCs the ranking depends on, and exits non-zero on disagreement. Code and cached activations will be released on acceptance; they are omitted here only because a repository link would deanonymise us.
